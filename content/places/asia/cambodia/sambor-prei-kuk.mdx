A pre-Angkorian temple city scattered through the forest near Kampong Thom, brick sanctuaries older than Angkor itself, left half-swallowed by tree roots and shade. Quieter and far less crowded than the big-name sites further north, and the better for it.

## Sights & Culture

Sambor Prei Kuk was the 7th-century capital of Chenla, built under King Ishanavarman I (r. 616–637 CE) and dedicated to Shiva, predating Angkor by some three centuries. It's now a UNESCO World Heritage Site, made up of several distinct temple groups (Prasat Sambor, Prasat Tao, and others) connected by forest paths. It's much quieter than Angkor, with far fewer tourists, and though less impressive in scale, the age adds something.

The temples are brick rather than the sandstone of later Khmer architecture, with sandstone reserved for decorative elements: door frames, lintels, colonettes, and moonstones. Some structures are rectangular, others octagonal; the overall effect, in the shade of tall trees, is more archaeological wander than monument tour.

### Prasat Sambor (the North Group)

Prasat Sambor centres on a main tower (N1) with doorways facing all four cardinal directions and a corner tower at each corner of the terrace. An inscription (K.436) found on the outer eastern gate names an officer, Vikramasimgha, and references temple worship dating back to the reign of Yasovarman I (r. 889–900 CE) — evidence the site stayed in active use well into the Angkorian period.

### Ashram Maha Rusei

Ashram Maha Rusei (N17) is a rare monolithic sandstone cellar, a single assembled block rather than built-up brick. Likely 7th-century and Gupta-influenced. Locals call it "Kuk Vihear" ("temple of a monastery"), reflecting its later Theravada Buddhist use from the 15th–19th centuries. A 2022–23 conservation project uncovered a two-tier brick platform beneath it that Henri Parmentier had documented decades earlier but which had gone unexcavated.

### Prasat Srang Preah

Prasat Srang Preah, dedicated to Brahma, sits on a raised terrace with three false doors and a carved eastern lintel in early Prei Kmeng style (mid-7th century). A Brahma statue found nearby in 1916, four-faced, arms and feet missing, now lives in the National Museum in Phnom Penh. Villagers still hold rain-calling ceremonies here in times of drought, pouring water over the temple's drainage channel.

### The Main Sanctuary (Prasat Tao)

The Main Sanctuary (Prasat Tao / S1 group) rises 22 metres and is dedicated to Shiva, with lintels depicting Indra on the east face and a dancing Shiva on the west. Sandstone lions once guarded the stair newels; several were removed for safekeeping during the unrest and have since been partially reinstalled, including one lion in 2023 that conservators realised had been placed in the wrong spot for decades.

### Conservation and the site today

The site's 20th-century history is layered onto the ancient one: wartime occupation from the 1970s to 1990s left bullet holes, bomb craters, and looting pits (one, at Ashram Maha Rusei, was dug three metres deep). Site boards are unusually candid about this, including a note that the ground in front of the S1 sanctuary was once an actual bomb crater. Conservation, led since the mid-1990s by Cambodia's Ministry of Culture and the National Authority for Sambor Prei Kuk (NASPK), with support from EFEO, UNESCO, and foreign grants (India funded the 2022–23 Ashram Maha Rusei project), is ongoing. You'll likely see active restoration work, scaffolding, or hard-hatted teams as you go.

Worth reading the info boards properly rather than walking past — they're detailed, well-illustrated with archival photos going back to 1916, and give a genuinely rare warts-and-all account of both the ancient building and the modern damage and repair.

It's easy to miss temples if you're not paying attention — the groups are spread out through genuine forest, not manicured lawn, and some structures are little more than a brick mound with a signboard. Worth taking your time rather than rushing between the "main" towers.
