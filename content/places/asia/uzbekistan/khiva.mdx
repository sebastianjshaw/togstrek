Khiva is the Silk Road city that time forgot. The Itchan Kala, the walled inner city, is a UNESCO World Heritage Site that exists as a lived-in medieval city. Mudbrick walls, narrow alleys, tiled minarets, and the accumulated weight of a place that was a significant centre of Islamic scholarship, trade, and, for a period, one of Central Asia's most notorious slave markets.

---

## Sights & Culture

### Pahlavan Mahmud Mausoleum

The mausoleum of Khiva's patron saint, Pahlavan Mahmud, a 14th-century wrestler, poet, and fur trader who died at 79 having never married, his living arrangements remain, as the guide put it, unclear. He was celebrated in his lifetime for both his physical strength and his verse, and the mausoleum reflects the esteem in which Khiva has held him ever since. The tilework inside is among the finest in the city.

Pahlavan exemplified the quote "The nation that will insist on drawing a broad line of demarcation between the fighting man and the thinking man is liable to find its fighting done by fools and its thinking by cowards." - Lt. Gen. Sir William Francis Butler

### Allakuli Khan Caravanserai

One of the old trading stops (Caravanserai) within the walled city, where merchants and their goods would have rested on the Silk Road routes. The structure gives a reasonable sense of the commercial scale that once moved through Khiva.

### Kalta Minor Minaret

A landmark minaret within the Itchan Kala with beautiful blue tilework.

### Juma Minaret

The minaret standing beside the Juma Mosque, built in the 18th century by the court official Abdurrahman Mihtar to replace an earlier structure. At around 30 metres it's modest by Khiva standards, and deliberately plain: unlike its turquoise-tiled neighbours it carries almost no decoration, just an eight-arched lantern and a stalactite cornice. It's one of five minarets spaced roughly 200 metres apart across the old city, each marking a different landmark.

### Juma Mosque

Central Asia's most unusual mosque interior: a single flat-roofed hall held up by 213 carved wooden columns, with no dome, no portal, and no courtyard. The columns were assembled across centuries rather than built at once, several bear carved dates (1316, 1510, 1788, 1789), and the carving style shifts accordingly, from deep Kufic script on the oldest pillars to later geometric and floral patterns. Light comes from small openings cut into the roof, keeping the hall dim; the walls carry faded black-and-red paintings of trees and irises, said to symbolise peace.

### Kunya-Ark

Khiva's citadel, founded in 1686 by Arang Khan at the western edge of the Itchan Kala and the seat of its khans into the 20th century. Much of the original complex was destroyed during an 18th-century Persian invasion, so most of what's standing now dates to a 19th-century rebuild: a throne room with a silver-clad wooden throne made in 1816, the khan's mint, a mosque with a gold-on-blue ceiling, and a harem added under Muhammad Rahim Khan II. The throne room's tiled aivan is worth lingering over.

### Muhammad Amin Khan Madrasa

Built between 1852 and 1855, this is the largest madrasa in Central Asia, with room for 250 students; part of the city wall was reportedly demolished to make space for it. It now operates as the Orient Star Hotel, with guests sleeping in the old double-roomed student cells, a conversion UNESCO has been openly uneasy about.

### Shergazi Khan Madrasa

Khiva's oldest, built between 1719 and 1726, reputedly using Persian prisoners of war as labour in exchange for their eventual freedom. It earned the nickname "Maskan-i fazilan", the abode of the educated, because so many of its students went on to become noted poets and scholars, among them the Turkmen poet Makhtumkuli. A single-storey building around a four-aivan courtyard, with 55 student cells still intact.

### Bagcha Darvoza

The north gate of the Itchan Kala, its name meaning roughly "garden gate". A 19th-century structure, twin-domed, with guard rooms on either side that once housed sentries and customs officials checking goods and travellers passing through. It was rebuilt in 1959-60 under Soviet restoration and again in 1997 after independence.

### Tash Khauli Palace

Built between 1830 and 1841 for Allakuli Khan, who wanted somewhere grander than the ageing Kunya-Ark. More than 260 rooms are arranged around three courtyards: the harem, finished first, with four lodges for the khan's wives and one larger one for himself; the Ichrat Khauli, where he received guests and ambassadors; and the Arz Khauli, the court of justice, the largest of the three. The blue-and-white majolica tilework throughout is by the court ceramicist Abdullah Jin. According to local accounts, the first architect assigned to the project was executed after voicing doubts it could ever be finished, a story worth taking with some scepticism but repeated by enough guides to be part of the palace's own folklore.

### Caravan monument

A bronze sculpture group near the old city walls: a line of laden camels led by two figures in traditional dress, a fairly literal nod to Khiva's centuries as a Silk Road stopover. Not a subtle piece of public art, but a popular photo stop and a reasonable prompt for explaining what actually moved through the city in its trading heyday.

## Museums & Galleries

### The Exhibition of Akmechet Mennonites

An unexpected and well-curated exhibition covering a largely forgotten chapter of Central Asian history. In the late 19th century, a group of Russian Mennonite families moved to Uzbekistan specifically to avoid military conscription, a pacifist community seeking the edges of the empire. The exhibition documents their lives through artefacts, rare photographs, and video, covering their agricultural practices, community values, and the distinct cultural identity they maintained in an environment that had almost nothing in common with their origins. It's the kind of small museum that justifies its existence entirely, and a useful counterpoint to the Islamic architecture surrounding it.

## Shopping

### Woodshop

Traditional Khivan woodcarving, a craft the city has been known for across the Silk Road period and beyond. Worth a look even if you're not buying. There are any number of them around, look outside the main streets for less openly touristy products.

### Khiva Silk Workshop

A working carpet and suzani (embroidered textile) workshop, set up in 2001 as a joint project between the NGO Operation Mercy and UNESCO, with a dedicated suzani workshop following in 2004 with the British Council. The point is twofold: revive traditional Khivan patterns and natural dyeing techniques, and provide steady, fairly paid work in a city where that's not a given. Watching the weaving in progress makes the finished carpets in the Carpet Museum below make a lot more sense.

### Carpet Museum

Part museum, part shop, the carpets here are the regional style and the context the museum provides makes the shopping more informed.
