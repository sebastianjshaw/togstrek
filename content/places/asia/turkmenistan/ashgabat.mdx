Ashgabat means City of Love. All buildings are white and so are all of the cars. It’s a peculiar feeling, making the whole city feel like a film set or something not quite real. The First and Second Presidents did this for a sense of purity. It’s got the nickname "Ash-vegas" because not only are all the buildings white, they are all lined with LEDs putting on complex showings at night its stunning but hides a depressing truth. One reason for the LED show is to hide how few of the rooms are lit. The city is huge and the average apartment size is 100sq meters with an average price of 80,000 usd. This is very affordable with a 1% interest rate mortgage. Despite this, the city is underpopulated.

The city is full of strange buildings, a haven for architects to really let themselves express unique ideas. The airport in shape of falcon, there is a fully enclosed ferris wheel, and a building of multiple interlocking cubes.

## Sights & Culture

### Changing of the Guards

I had the chance to watch this ceremonial display, marked by sharp precision and an undercurrent of national pride. There’s a sense of formality and spectacle in the air that makes the moment unforgettable and a sense you are being watched as much as they are.

### Constitution Monument

A towering modern structure that seems to reach for the sky. Nearby stands an “alien egg” with a design rumoured to be inspired by the Burj Dubai though it is unclear quite how.

### Wedding Palace

Don’t let the simple cube structure fool you, this building, dedicated to marriage ceremonies, it is impressive, but its interior is empty, like many of the impressive buildings in Ashgabat. Its exterior might be boxy, but it’s a hub of celebration and tradition.

### Ferris Wheel

Billed as the largest indoor Ferris wheel in the world, this is Ashgabat’s statement piece for thrill-seekers and tourists alike. It’s a curious sight, part amusement park, part architectural marvel. Its claim to fame was added after the fact, as it turns out it was not the largest outdoor wheel they built a container over it and turned it into the largest indoor wheel. It does not run generally, you have to call ahead and book a place.

### Nisa

Nisa sits just outside Ashgabat, a UNESCO World Heritage site that once served as a royal seat of the Parthian Empire. The Parthians emerged in the 3rd century BC and built a power base that stretched from Central Asia to the Mediterranean. Nisa was at the heart of that story.

The site is divided into Old Nisa and New Nisa, two fortified settlements on low hills overlooking the Kopetdag mountains. Today you walk among broken walls, collapsed towers, and the outlines of ceremonial halls and storerooms. The scale alone tells you this was no village. It was a capital that managed armies, trade routes, and diplomacy across vast distances.

Excavations here have uncovered huge wine jars, carved ivory drinking horns, and sealed clay documents. These finds paint a picture of a courtly life filled with ritual, record keeping, and luxury goods. Many of the most spectacular pieces now rest in the National Museum in Ashgabat, but standing on the site where they were found gives them new weight.

The experience of visiting Nisa is raw and unpolished. There are no reconstructions, just windswept mud-brick ruins under the Turkmen sun. Birds circle above. The silence is heavy. Yet once you start to imagine the place alive with priests, soldiers, and merchants, the ruins transform. Nisa was more than a stronghold. It was a centre of empire and a hub of the Silk Road.

### Memorial Complex

A solemn homage to the past. The complex houses monuments dedicated to significant events in Turkmen history, encouraging reflection on the country’s journey to independence.

### Dog Statue

A monument celebrating the local Alabay dog breed, revered for its loyalty and strength. It’s a photogenic spot and a chance to see Turkmen pride in a surprisingly playful form.

### Independence Square

A sprawling plaza marking Turkmenistan’s independence in 1991. It’s a gathering place for national events and parades, framed by fountains and grand government buildings.

### Library

I didn’t have a chance to explore inside, but the exterior is enough to intrigue any bibliophile. One day, I’d love to discover what stories it holds within those sleek, white walls.

## Museums & Galleries

### National Museum

The guide here had a habit of answering most questions with a noncommittal “Maybe,” giving the place an air of mystery. Despite the coy responses, the museum’s exhibits provide a fascinating glimpse into Turkmenistan’s past. If you can decipher the maybes, that is.

Walking into the National Museum in Ashgabat feels like stepping through time, each gallery peeling back another layer of Central Asia’s deep history. What struck me most was how clearly the story of Turkmenistan’s people has been laid out, from the first hunters to the builders of vast Bronze Age settlements.

It begins in the Paleolithic, where flint tools and cave paintings speak of a world dominated by the hunt. The walls show goats, gazelles, even mammoths, sketched by hands that believed drawing could bring luck on the chase. It’s raw, simple, but it feels alive, like you can still sense the urgency of survival in those lines.

By the Mesolithic, people were hunting with bone-tipped spears, fishing with harpoons, and carving needles from animal bones. In the Djebel caves, archaeologists found heaps of wild animal remains, sheep, goats, even wild cats, proof that life revolved around the hunt. Yet, tiny microlith tools hint at something more refined, a careful craftsmanship that was beginning to change how people lived.

Then comes the Neolithic revolution. Here, in the foothills of the Kopetdag mountains, wild wheat and spring waters created the first fertile plots. People settled, kept animals, and began farming. No longer wanderers, they were laying down roots, literally, and you can almost picture those first small fields bursting with green.

The Jeytun Culture, just outside Ashgabat, takes this further. Square clay houses, storerooms, utility spaces, the beginnings of village life. Standing in front of the exhibits, I could see the transition: tribes becoming farmers, hunters becoming herders, and with that, the foundations of civilisation in Central Asia.

The Eneolithic period brings a leap forward: copper axes, jewellery, two-edged knives, all alongside beautifully decorated ceramics. The invention of the plough here at Namazga-depe is a quiet revolution, a tool that changed how humans worked the land forever. I couldn’t help but think of how many generations have turned soil since that first design.

Finally, the museum unveils the Bronze Age cities, Anau, Namazga, Kara-depe, Yylgynly-depe. These weren’t just villages anymore; they were hubs of trade and culture. Painted pottery, monumental burials, even evidence of links to Mesopotamia and Elam. Walking past these displays, you realise Turkmenistan wasn’t some isolated steppe but a beating heart of early civilisation, part of a vast network that shaped the ancient world.

## Parks & Gardens

### Independence Park

I wandered through Independence Park alone, everyone else was happier to take the bus back to the hotel, but I decided I’d rather take the slow way and see more of Ashgabat. Walking through this park in the centre of the city, I spotted three cleaners, five gardeners, and a lone local who kindly pointed out a towering statue shaped like a giant book. Meanwhile, one hapless driver wrestled with a broken-down car. Other than myself, I didn’t see anyone actually enjoying the park. I think it’s a pretty good reflection of the city as a whole, it’s almost a set-piece something designed to look like a city in the hopes of bringing people to it.

## Restaurants & Bars

### Shopping Centre Restaurant

Deep inside a busy mall, we found the sole eatery serving alcohol, decked out in a bizarre mix of Italian décor and pirate ship themes. Surreal? Absolutely.

### Sport Hotel

A favourite meeting spot for locals and travellers alike, it offers a glimpse into the city’s social scene. It’s a beautiful hotel and an exact duplicate sits over the road.

### Sa Kofe

Tucked away with a somewhat hidden entrance, Sa Kofe is worth seeking out. It’s cosy, relaxed, and perfect for a mid-afternoon caffeine fix or a quiet chat after a long day of exploring.

### Birk Garden Pub

Set outdoors amidst flowing water features, this pub serves a wildly creative “shepherd’s pie pizza.” It’s hearty, comforting, and odd. By this point, we were all counting our bank notes, as this was our final meal together before flights home early that morning in most cases, so we were ensuring we had enough for dinner and perhaps a coffee at the airport as there was no way, at this stage, to get more money.

## Shopping

### Russian Bazaar

The main market in Ashgabat is the Russian bazaar, a sprawling collection of outside stalls and indoor kiosks. You can buy food, drinks, random items and some souvenirs, but not many as tourism just isn’t sizable enough to depend on as an income stream. When we were there there were extra stalls for back to school supplies to get the students ready. The lively chatter and constant haggling make it an entertaining spot to people-watch, but there’s not really anywhere we found to sit down and do that without being very obvious.

### Cable Car

I didn’t manage to ride it, but I saw the restaurant perched at the top of a darkened hill, glowing like a beacon in the night. Next time, I’ll definitely hop aboard to see the city’s sea of white buildings stretching out below.
